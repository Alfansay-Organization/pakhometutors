Why Cramming the Night Before Feels Effective — Until the Exam Starts

Cramming produces a short-lived sense of mastery. You have just seen the material; everything feels close to the surface. Sleep, stress, and a differently worded question often push that material out of reach by the next afternoon.

What spacing does differently

Spaced practice spreads the same total study time across several days. Each time you return to a topic after a gap, retrieval is slightly harder — and that difficulty is useful. The brain must rebuild the path to the answer, which makes the path more stable.

A practical weekly pattern

  • Day 1: Learn the topic and do 5 mixed questions.
  • Day 2: Closed-book recall of key points + 3 new questions.
  • Day 4: Short mixed quiz including this topic and an older one.
  • Day 7: One exam-style question under timed conditions.

You are not studying more hours; you are placing the hours where memory research says they count.

Interleaving beats blocked practice

Doing twenty similar algebra questions in a row feels smooth. Mixing algebra, geometry, and a word problem feels harder — and prepares you better for real papers, where question types jump around.

One honest test

If you can only solve a question when it sits next to five identical examples, you have practised recognition of a pattern in context, not independent problem solving. Move that question to a mixed sheet and try again after a day.
