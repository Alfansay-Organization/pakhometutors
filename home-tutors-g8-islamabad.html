Teach It in One Minute: The Feynman Check for Any School Topic

If you cannot explain an idea in simple language, you probably do not understand it yet — even if you can recite the textbook definition. Physicist Richard Feynman’s study method is built on that uncomfortable truth.

The one-minute version for school work

  1. Choose one idea (for example: photosynthesis, Pythagoras’ theorem, or a grammar rule).
  2. Set a timer for sixty seconds.
  3. Explain the idea out loud or on paper as if teaching a Class 6 student.
  4. Whenever you use a heavy word, stop and replace it with a plainer one.
  5. Circle every place where you hesitated or said “you know what I mean.”

Those circles are your real syllabus for the next study block.

Example: explaining density without jargon

Weak version: “Density is mass per unit volume.”

Clearer version: “Density tells you how tightly matter is packed. If two objects have the same size, the heavier one is denser.”

If you can add one everyday example — “a steel spoon feels heavier than a plastic spoon of similar size” — the idea becomes harder to forget.

Why this helps exams

Board and Cambridge questions increasingly ask for explanation, not only recall. A student who has practised simple explanations writes clearer “describe” and “explain” answers. The same habit also reveals false confidence before the exam, when there is still time to fix gaps.

Parents supporting learners with home tutors in G-8 Islamabad can ask after each topic: “Can you teach that back in one minute?” It is a fast diagnostic that needs no special materials.

Rule of thumb

If your explanation needs the textbook open beside you, you are still in the reading stage. Close the book, try again, and only then check what you missed.
